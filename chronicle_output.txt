## Halveth

The vessel has run aground on a cold shore. This is where the fifty stand. This is where they begin.

Rovan Halveth does not sleep when the others sleep. He sits at the rail of the beached hull and watches the coast in the grey early light. The water is still. The land beyond the tideline offers nothing yet — no smoke, no movement, no sign that anything here has been waiting for them. He watches it anyway.

The hunger in him is not new. He carries it the way a man carries a stone he has carried long enough to forget it is a stone.

Brael Halveth stays close to the hull. Rovan sees this. He does not speak to her. They share a name and a distance, and the distance is not new either. Keth Halveth moves near the stern, crouched over something that may be rope or may be nothing. Rovan does not go to him.

The outcasts keep to themselves — Fen Caelder, Aldun Ironveth, Tack Worvane — gathered at the low end of the deck where the hull has listed into the sand. They are hungry. They do not complain where Rovan can hear them. He notes this. A man who does not complain is either resigned or waiting.

Vael Caelder stands apart from the others and looks inland. She is the only one looking inland.

Rovan watches her look. The plains beyond the shore are wide and pale under winter sky. He thinks nothing that the chronicle can record. He watches.

The day has not yet asked anything of them. He knows this will not last.

## Torcren

Cress Torcren is the first to step off the vessel onto the shore.

He does not announce this. He climbs down the hull where the sand has banked against it and he stands on the new ground and he looks. The coast runs north and south. Inland, the plain opens flat and cold under cloud. There are no trees close. There is no water visible. There is wind.

He turns back toward the vessel. Fifty people are on it or near it. He counts, not because he doubts, but because he is the kind of man who counts.

Ovra Torcren watches him from the rail. She is hungry and says nothing. Theron Torcren has not looked up. Corra Torcren sits with her back against the hull, tired in a way that is not only last night's tired.

Maren Stonefall stands at the prow. She is the leader. She watches the shore the way a leader watches — not with curiosity, but with the weight of consequence. She has not yet spoken this morning. The others wait without appearing to wait.

Balt Ironveth crouches near the waterline, testing the sand with one hand. He is a survivor, the data says. His hands look like it.

Rovan Halveth is at the rail on the far side, looking the other direction.

Cress walks a few paces further onto the shore. The ground holds him. He crouches and takes a handful of the cold soil and lets it fall.

Nothing is decided yet. The plain waits. The fifty wait. Cress Torcren stands between them and it, and does not yet know that this is what he does.

## Halveth

The second day comes the way the first ended — cold, grey, without announcement.

Rovan Halveth is still at the rail. He has slept in the way a man sleeps who does not trust sleep. An hour, perhaps. Perhaps less. His eyes are open now and the shore is what they find.

Nothing on it has changed.

The fifty are slow to rise. They are hungry, and hunger in the second morning of a new place carries a different weight than the first. The first morning there is still the journey in the body. The second morning there is only the place.

Brael Halveth has moved no farther from the hull than she moved yesterday. She sits near the midship boards with her knees drawn up. She does not look at Rovan and he does not look at her. The name between them is the full extent of what they share, and it has always been enough — enough to keep them proximate, not enough to make them close.

Keth crouches near the stern again. The rope from yesterday, or the nothing from yesterday. Rovan watches him without watching.

The outcasts have not dispersed. Fen Caelder, Aldun Ironveth, Tack Worvane — they remain at the low end of the deck where the hull lists into sand. Hungry, the three of them. Quiet, the three of them. A man who does not complain on the second morning is no longer waiting. He has made a decision that is not yet visible.

Vael Caelder stands where she stood. She is still looking inland.

This is what Rovan notices: she has not gone. She has looked at the plains since before the light had color and she has not moved toward them and she has not moved away from them. There is a kind of person who looks at a thing long enough that the looking becomes intention. Rovan is one such person. He recognizes the other kind from a distance.

The shore does not offer food. It does not offer shelter or fire or certainty. It offers the same pale expanse it offered yesterday, and the day will ask them to reckon with that sooner than they have reckoned with it.

Rovan descends from the rail. His feet find the sand. He stands on the new ground and he is not the first to have stood on it — that was Cress Torcren, yesterday, a fact the chronicle has noted — but he stands on it now, and the ground is cold, and it holds him.

He does not go far. Five paces from the hull. He looks at the tideline. He looks at what the tide has left.

He begins to think about what can be eaten here. This is not new behavior in a man. It is the oldest.

## Torcren

This is the second time he has stood here. The ground is the same ground. He knows it differently now — he has slept on what it cost him to stand here the first time, and he is still here, which is its own kind of answer.

The plains run inland without invitation. Flat, pale, winter-stripped. No tree within reach of the eye. No water he can name. The wind comes off the land this morning rather than the sea, which is a different cold, drier, with less mercy in it.

Ovra Torcren comes down behind him. She does not speak. She stands beside him and looks where he looks. She is hungry — he knows this because he is hungry and they arrived on the same vessel with the same provisions that ended the same day before. She says nothing about it. This is Ovra's way, and Cress has learned the shape of her silences well enough to read them. This one says: *what do you see.*

He says: nothing yet.

She does not leave.

Maren Stonefall has come to the prow of the vessel. She stands above them both, looking down at the shore, then out at the plain. She is the leader. Her silences are heavier than other people's silences. She watches Cress the way a leader watches a man who has already stepped off the edge — not to pull him back, but to see how far he falls and whether the falling teaches anything.

Cress has felt her watching since yesterday. He does not know yet what to do with it.

## Halveth

The third morning carries no more promise than the second. The cold has settled into the hull planks. It will not leave.

Rovan stands five paces from the ship and looks at what the tide has brought. Kelp in dark ropes. A shell the size of a fist, pale and ribbed. Small creatures in the shallows that move when his shadow crosses them. He crouches. He watches them. He does not yet know their name in this place, but he knows the shape of a thing that can be eaten.

He does not eat. Not yet. He is counting them first.

Behind him the fifty stir. He hears it without turning — the sounds a hungry camp makes, which are not many sounds, and none of them urgent. Brael is still at the hull. Keth has not moved from the stern. The three at the low end of the deck — Fen Caelder, Aldun Ironveth, Tack Worvane — sit as they sat yesterday, which is to say they sit the way men sit when they have run out of things to do with their hands except wait.

Vael Caelder has moved.

This is the change of the third morning. She stood two days at the rail and looked at the plains and now she has descended to the sand, and she is not looking at the plains from the sand — she is walking toward them. Slowly. Not urgently. The way a person walks when the decision is already made and the body is only now following.

Rovan watches her go. He does not call after her. It is not his place and he knows it. But he marks the distance between her and the hull, and he marks how that distance grows.

She goes perhaps forty paces. She stops. She bends down. She looks at something in the grass where the sand gives way.

She does not come back. Not yet.

Rovan returns to the shallows.


## Torcren

Cress Torcren was the first to stand on this ground. He has not forgotten it. It sits in him the way a first thing sits — not heavily, but present, a stone in shallow water.

This morning he walks the tideline. He is hungry in the clean way a strong man is hungry, which is to say it sharpens him rather than dulling him. He moves east along the shore, away from the cluster of the ship, and he looks at what is here.

He finds what Rovan has already found: the kelp, the shells, the creatures in the shallows. He finds also a line of stones where the coast turns, and past the stones a place where a small stream cuts through the sand to reach the sea. Fresh water. He stands at it. He cups his hands and drinks.

It is cold and it is clean.

He looks back at the ship. It is smaller from here. The fifty look like a single dark thing against the pale wood.

Ovra Torcren is somewhere in that dark thing. They share a name and share a history that the name does not contain. She has not come with him this morning, and he has not asked her to. This is the shape of them.

Theron Torcren is there also, and Corra Torcren, who is Theron's and not truly his — she carries the name through the marriage that ended before this shore — and Cress watches the ship the way a man watches a thing he is responsible for and does not know how to say so.

Maren Stonefall stands at the rail. The chronicle has not spoken of her before now, but she has been here since the beginning. She stands differently than the others stand. She stands as though the rail belongs to her, or she to it — as though authority, even here, even hungry, does not leave the body.

Cress marks her.

He fills his hands with water once more. Then he turns back toward the ship. He has something to bring them now. It is small, a stream, a direction, a fact about this coast. But the third morning has made it.

He walks back. The ground holds him. He lets it.

This is what the fourth morning makes: a root, unnamed, and two people looking at it.

Brael has come down from the hull by now. Brael does most things slowly and this is not slowness, it is his nature — the way he takes a step before he takes a step, the mind arriving after the body. Keth remains at the stern. Keth has not moved far in four days and this is its own kind of statement.

The three — Fen, Aldun, Tack — have come down to the sand. They sit on the sand now rather than the deck. This is a small migration. It may mean nothing. There is fresh water to the east and there are creatures in the shallows and there is a woman who pulled a root from the ground. These are the facts of the fourth morning. They are small. ## Halveth

The root sits on the sand between them.

Vael put it down at some point in the early morning and no one has moved it. It does not look like food. It looks like a thing that might become food, which is different, which is the kind of difference this place insists on.

Rovan is in the shallows.

He has been there long enough that his feet are past cold and into something else, something the body accepts when the body has decided to accept it. He watches the creatures beneath the surface. Small things, pale-bellied, moving in the particular way that means they do not know he is there yet. He has learned the difference. He does not know when he learned it.

This is what the endurance has become: not the capacity to suffer but the capacity to remain. To stay in the cold water until the cold water is no longer what the mind is occupied with. To watch until watching becomes a kind of knowledge. His body is consuming itself in the patient way of a fire that has found green wood, and still he does not leave the shallows.

Brael stands on the sand at the waterline. He came down slowly, in his way — the way of a man whose thoughts arrive after his steps — and now he stands and watches Rovan and says nothing. Brael has learned something in four days, which is when Rovan goes still it is not helplessness. It is the opposite of helplessness.

Keth has not moved from the stern. The chronicle notes this the way it notes weather.

Vael Caelder sits near the root. She brought it from the grass and set it down and now she sits near it and does not eat it and does not explain herself. Fen is beside her. Aldun is nearby. Tack is a few paces further, sitting with his forearms on his knees, watching the water. The three of them chose the sand on the fourth morning and they are still on the sand now and still close to Vael, which may be proximity or may be something taking shape beneath proximity.

Rovan moves.

It is not a sudden thing. He shifts his weight, lowers his hands beneath the surface, holds absolutely still. The creatures do not scatter. He has solved that part. What he has not yet solved is the taking. His hands come up empty. He looks at them. He goes back to watching.

The hunger in him is not a small thing. But it does not reach his face.

## Halveth

Rovan comes out of the water.

Not because he has caught anything. Not because the cold has finally broken through the place where the body accepts it. He comes out because something has changed in the way he stands in it, and the change is complete, and he knows this the way he knows when a fire is ready and when it is not.

He stands on the wet sand. Brael watches him. Rovan looks at his hands.

He goes back in.

This is what the endurance has become: not the second attempt but the fact that the second attempt is the same as the first. The weight of failure does not accumulate the way it does in ordinary men. It moves through him and leaves. He lowers his hands. He waits. The pale-bellied creatures turn in the cold water beneath them and do not scatter, and this is still the part he has solved, and the part he has not solved is still the taking, and he stays with what he has not solved the way a stone stays in a river.

Brael does not leave the waterline.

He has been standing there long enough that the tide has come to his feet and gone again. He brought nothing with him when he came down. He has nothing to do but watch, and watching is what he does, with the patience of a man who has decided that what Rovan is doing matters even though he cannot yet say why.

Keth has not moved from the stern. The chronicle notes this again.

Vael Caelder is no longer sitting near the root.
